# Plan and current position

The five steps, what is done, and what is next. Kept short and kept true: if a line here says done, the
evidence is a green CI run or a byte-level test.

## Step 1 — the whole country, in 3D

**Data (done).** The compiler fetches the Qatar OpenStreetMap extract and the SRTM elevation tiles,
projects everything into EPSG:32639, and writes the bundles described in `docs/WORLD_FORMATS.md`.
The real country now compiles end to end in CI: 186,993 building footprints (2,137 of them named),
179,943 road ways, 5,784 land-use polygons, 1,130 water polygons, 421 barriers, 2,254 points of
interest, 16,780 strings, 11,502 km² of land. Every file is hashed into the manifest and every bundle
is verified on load.

**Geometry (done, tested).** Terrain, roads, building extrusions and land-use colouring are generated by
meshers that have no OpenGL in them at all, so their output is unit-tested: triangle counts, winding,
roof shapes, road widths, and that a chunk's mesh really covers its own rectangle.

**Drawing (done, compiles).** `WorldRenderer` is the only class in the project that talks to OpenGL:
it uploads finished vertex arrays and issues draw calls, with one shader that branches on surface type
(land, water, wall, roof, road, marking) and a sky drawn as a direction rather than as geometry.

**Streaming (done).** The country is cut into 500 m chunks, meshed around the player, nearest first,
with per-chunk level of detail and an LRU cache. The floating origin rebases under the player so metres
stay small at the far end of the country.

**Remaining for step 1:** run it on a device. Shaders are never compiled locally (no GPU, no JDK here),
so the first on-device run is the real test of the GLSL. Everything that can be checked without a GPU is
checked.

## Step 2 — human, car, aeroplane, helicopter, boat, with physics

**Bodies (written, evidence in CI).** Each of the five is now a real body in
`core/src/main/java/ai/arena/qatar/vehicle/`, integrated at the fixed physics rate:

| Body | What it models | Numbers it is built from |
|---|---|---|
| `HumanBody` | walking, running, a 0.42 m step, jumping, wading | 82 kg, 1.5 m/s walk, 6.6 m/s run, 3.1 m/s take-off |
| `CarBody` | bicycle model, grip budget, power limit, soft-ground bogging | 1620 kg, 92 kW, 2.79 m wheelbase, 0.86 m² CdA |
| `AircraftBody` | trimmed angle of attack, stall and recovery, ground roll and rotation | 1043 kg, 16.2 m² wing, CL_max 1.51, 2.25 kN thrust |
| `HelicopterBody` | collective lag, cyclic tilt, rotor damping, tail-rotor yaw | 2200 kg, 5.6 m rotor, 1.9 × weight of thrust |
| `BoatBody` | heave with buoyancy and planing lift, Froude-number resistance, beaching | 1850 kg, 6.5 m hull, 3 kN of propeller |

`PlayerController` is the seam: it maps the HUD's axes onto each body's own axes, feeds it the world
through `WorldSurface` (terrain height, compiled material, water depth), and puts the camera where the
driver, pilot or walker would be. Walking turns the body, driving swings a chase camera, and the aircraft
have a self-centring stick that commands attitude and returns to trim.

**Terrain contact (done).** The bodies refuse to move over terrain that has not streamed in, which is what
stops a fast aeroplane from flying through an unloaded chunk.

**Walls (done).** Buildings are solid from the ground to their compiled roof height, tested at the
altitude the body is at, so a walker and a car are stopped by a wall and a helicopter above a tower is
not. The walker refuses the step; the car tests its own nose. The footprint is treated as a box, which
is written down in the code as the limit of the test: a courtyard is walkable through its bounding box.

**Evidence.** `VehicleDynamicsTest`, `BuildingCollisionTest` and `MapTeleportTest` run in the compile
gate: 78 tests, 0 failures, on the commit that added the walls.

**Barriers (done).** Walls, fences and hedges are solid along their own line segments, from the ground
to their compiled height: a person and a car are stopped, a gate is a way through, a kerb is a step you
take, and an aircraft passing over a 2.4 m wall is not in the wall's way. Getting there turned up a bug
worth writing down: each barrier's bounding box was seeded from its absolute first vertex and then from
its origin-relative ones, so a Doha wall claimed to begin at EPSG east 553,100 in a world 259 km wide —
which is why no barrier in the country had ever been meshed either. The box is in one frame now.

**Remaining for step 2:** collisions between vehicles, and the in-cockpit feel on a real device.

## The vertex layout, which was wrong in a way nothing local could see

The layout declared the auxiliary vertex data as four packed bytes -- a 28-byte vertex -- while every
mesher wrote four floats, 40 bytes. libGDX takes the stride and the attribute offsets from that
declaration, so the driver would have been told each vertex began twelve bytes before it did. The
shader then made a second, independent mistake with the same data: it divided by 255 what the meshers
had written as plain numbers, so a wall (written as 2.0) read as 510, fell past every branch of the
palette, and the compiled material ids of the whole country -- sabkha, mangrove, tidal mud, urban pad --
were never used. The tint every mesher computes per vertex was read by nothing at all.

Fixed in "Tell the driver the truth about the vertex stride": the layout is eleven floats, the ids are
read as numbers, land vertices carry their material id, and the tint multiplies. `VertexLayoutTest`
asserts the three-way agreement -- floats written, stride declared, shader read -- because that is the
kind of mismatch a GPU-less CI can still catch if somebody writes the assertion down.

## Step 3 — detail assets

Walls band their glazing procedurally in metres, so detail does not swim. Road ribbons are drawn with
painted lane markings, and barriers (walls, railings, fences) extrude with the buildings.

**The bodies exist now.** `BodyMesher` builds the five shapes the player is -- a person, a car, an
aeroplane, a helicopter, a boat -- in metres, in the body's own frame, from the published dimensions of
real machines: 1.75 m of person, a 4.60 m car with a 2.79 m wheelbase, an 11 m wing, a 5.6 m rotor disc,
a 6.50 m hull that floats with 0.35 m of itself under the waterline. Each part carries a paint id
(car paint, glass, tyre, aircraft white, teak deck, skin, steel, rotor), so one surface type is silver on
one part and black on the next without a texture file anywhere in the project.

They are drawn in third person: the **VIEW** button swings the camera onto a boom behind the body, and
`PlayerController` keeps the camera's own orbit on top of it. In first person nothing of the player is
drawn, which is honest -- there is nothing to see -- and the button is one press away.

**The bodies move.** A part that turns is geometry built around its own pivot, placed by translating to
that pivot first and rotating second, so a wheel rolls on its axle instead of orbiting the car. Built
around, not centred on: a wheel belongs on its axle and would wobble anywhere else, while a leg belongs
*under* its hip, which is where most of a leg is. The same contract covers both -- the pivot is the one
point that does not move -- and `swingDegrees` is what tells a part that goes round from a part that
swings. The rates
are the machines' own: a car wheel at v/r (rolling without slipping), an aeroplane engine idling at 700 rpm
and turning 2,400 at full power, a rotor head governed near 330 rpm with a tail rotor 2.5 times as fast.
The car's running gear is now published by the mesher and held to `CarBody` by the test -- the same
0.33 m wheels, 1.57 m track and 2.79 m wheelbase -- because the old hardcoded 1.86 m track put the tyres
12 cm outside the bodywork. `BodyMesherTest` checks the shapes as shapes: dimensions against the published
figures, symmetry about the centreline, which end is the front, the hull bottom under the waterline, the
rotor clear above the cabin and below the fin, and every part's pivot holding still through a whole turn.
The symmetry assertion is scoped to the parts it is true of -- a wheel, a propeller, a rotor -- because
asserting it of a leg is asserting something nobody ever built, and a test that fails for a reason of its
own making costs a build.

**The text is an asset too.** The HUD fonts are generated by `tools/make_font.py` from the licensed
Amiri typeface, and the glyph set is the thing that decides whether a name prints or prints as a hole.
Held against the 17,718 names in the compiled country, the shipped atlases had no glyph for 'é', 'ā', 'ū'
or 'ی' -- nine names use them -- so the generator now covers every character class Amiri has in the ranges
the data uses (the rest of the Arabic block, Arabic Supplement, Latin-1 letters, Latin Extended-A, and the
punctuation the credits and instruments want) and drops duplicate code points instead of packing them
twice. 663 glyphs per font, and `FontCoverageTest` walks the shipped atlas with every string the game can
print, shaped.

**The credit is on the screen.** The attribution was in the compiled manifest from the start and never
drawn: ODbL asks a produced work to make attribution visible to the people using it, and a file inside the
APK does not do that. `Credits` holds the words, the map screen carries them in both languages, and the
first twelve seconds of a launch show them in the corner.

Still to add: doors and roof clutter on the buildings, kerbs, palms, window bands that vary by building
type, and a walking animation for the person, whose legs are still two static boxes.

## Step 4 — minimap, buttons, zoomable map, tap to travel

`MapRaster` paints the country once from the compiled data; the minimap and the full map are two crops of
that one texture, so they cannot disagree. `MapView` does pan/zoom about the fingers, clamped to the data.
`TeleportService` resolves a tap into a place that can be stood on: nearest named spawn point, else the
nearest road vertex, never the middle of the sea. The HUD draws a button for every locomotion type, and
taps are exact arithmetic on the same raster.

**One button, one light.** `HudLayout` used to keep a single "highlighted" field, so the six controls the
game sets state for every frame all wrote to the same flag and only the last one told the truth. Each
control now carries its own, and the locomotion buttons and the new VIEW button light up correctly.

**Every word in two languages.** The controls were already bilingual; the readout was not. The status
line now prints the locomotion, where the player is (on the ground, in the air, in the water), speed,
altitude and heading in Arabic under the English, shaped and reordered by `ArabicReshaper`, and the map's
hint is two lines so the reshapers never flip the English one. `BilingualStatusTest` holds the Arabic to
being Arabic, to carrying the same numbers, and to shaping into forms the shipped font carries. What
remains is a native speaker: the Arabic is written for this project and nobody has reviewed it.

**The map opens and closes, and says so.** The minimap carries "TAP TO OPEN MAP" and opens the full map
on a tap; the MAP button does the same from anywhere on the screen; the open map carries ZOOM + and -,
FIND ME, and CLOSE; and the phone's BACK key closes it too, caught with `setCatchKey(Keys.BACK, true)`
only while the map is up -- so outside the map, BACK still leaves the game as a player expects it to.
A teleport marks the *place* it went to, not the pixel that was touched: `MapView.screenXAt/screenYAt`
are the inverse of `eastAt/northAt`, so the crosshair stays on the destination through the recentring,
the panning and the zooming that follow. `drawTeleportMarker` had existed since step 4 and had never been
called once -- a marker with no call site is a feature that is not there.

**Tested.** `MapTeleportTest` holds the promises to the compiled geometry: pixels round trip to within a
cell, a place round trips back to its own pixel on the map (at every zoom: the marker must name the place
the player is standing on), water is water, a tap beside a primary road lands on that road, every tap over
the whole raster lands inside the country, every locomotion type has a start, and zoom holds the point
under the fingers still. It also caught two real bugs: inland water was outlined but never filled (so the raster believed a
lake was dry land), and a tap in the water with no harbour or road in reach resolved to the tapped point —
which is to say, into the sea. Water is filled now, and such a tap is moved to the nearest shore.

## Step 5 — the APK

Debug-signed APK, built and validated in CI (entry list, dex count, ABI coverage, bundle presence,
checksums), published on every green run to the rolling release
`v0.1.0-m0` so the download link never rots. The build now packages the country and starts in the
playable world; the remaining risk is what only a device can tell us (see open item 2).

## The first run on a phone

Installed on Android, the build showed a blue screen and nothing else. The world fragment shader declared
a variable called `half`, which GLSL ES 3.00 reserves -- so a spec-following driver rejects the shader,
the world program never links, and the renderer shut down quietly. The next frame then dereferenced a
null program and killed the GL thread, and the only place the reason existed was logcat.

Three things came out of it, and all three are in the build now:

  - `half` is `halfway`, and `ShaderSourcesTest` lints every shader: reserved words, 1.00 syntax in a
    300 es shader, a fragment shader without a float precision, calls to functions that are neither
    built in nor declared, and vertex/fragment varyings that do not match by name and type.
  - `ScreenLog` draws a console on the screen -- its own batch, its own font, three fallbacks -- and
    `QatarGame` runs the launch as stages across frames, printing each one as it starts and printing any
    failure with its stack frames and the GL strings. A crash is written to a file and printed by the
    next launch.
  - The map raster is built once, at 1024, in its own reported stage, and the spawn selection reuses it
    instead of building a second one.

## The second run on a phone

The diagnostics panel came back with the GL driver (Mali-G72, OpenGL ES 3.2), the manifest read out
correctly (world format 2, 259 x 268 km, 186,993 buildings, 179,943 roads), and three failures:

  - `world bundle missing: strings.bin.gz`. The Android asset tooling unpacks a `.gz` asset and drops the
    suffix, so the APK held `assets/world/strings.bin` with the inflated bytes while the manifest named
    `strings.bin.gz`. The country had never loaded on a device -- the whole file was in the package, one
    extension away from being found, and every test in this repository read the repository's own form of
    it. The loader resolves either name now, and accepts gzip or already-inflated contents, and
    `BundlePackagingTest` loads the world four ways while `validate_apk.sh` checks the artefact itself.
  - `HUD font unavailable`. `make_font.py` wrote `kernings count=0` before the character lines, and libGDX
    stops reading a `.fnt` at the first line that begins with `kernings`: zero glyphs parsed, a null
    glyph, an exception, and a HUD in the built-in ASCII font. Kerning is last now, the atlases are RGBA,
    and `FontCoverageTest` parses the shipped files the way libGDX does.
  - `demo terrain: L0001 ... u_cameraLocal ... The precision does not match`. An unqualified uniform is
    `highp` in a vertex stage and `mediump` in a fragment stage after `precision mediump float`; one
    uniform, two precisions, and a Mali driver will not link it. Everything crossing a stage boundary has
    an explicit precision now, held by `ShaderSourcesTest.precisionsMatch` and by `tools/check_shaders.py`.

Two tests turned out to have stopped looking at their subject (`VertexLayoutTest` matched no varyings at
all once qualifiers were added and passed on an empty set; `BundlePackagingTest` asserted a name against a
set that never contained it), and the first push of the round never reached the APK job because a test file
called `assertEquals` without importing it -- which is what `tools/check_java_symbols.py` now catches in a
second. 117 tests green, `validation=PASSED`, and the third install is the test of all of it.

Three more gates have since been written for the same reason -- each one is a mistake that cost a run
before it cost anything else. `tools/check_local_names.py` finds a local declared twice in one scope (the
`contact` in a test, which is the compiler's error and not the test's). `tools/check_float_narrowing.py`
finds a double-typed expression assigned to a float, which Java will not convert: on the road-clipping
push, `float worst = Math.max(worst, v - CHUNK_M)` was the only thing the compile gate had to say, and it
said it after six minutes of building. Its first draft read expressions as text and reported sixty-four
innocent lines -- every one of them a cast, a comparison, or a ternary -- so it was thrown away for one
that reads the parse tree and reports only what it can prove is a double. A gate that cries wolf is a gate
that gets switched off.

## The third run on a phone

The third install of the round-two build closed immediately. No panel, no message, nothing to
photograph -- which is itself information: the crash was happening after the last frame that could have
drawn a reason, or before the first one. The cause was in the chunk meshing path, and there were two
things wrong with it, one fatal and one that would have been fatal on a phone with less memory.

**The fatal one: a primitive started on a full mesh.** A GPU mesh addresses 65,000 vertices with 16-bit
indices. The meshers asked `MeshData.isFull()` -- which means *do four more vertices fit?* -- once before
adding a whole primitive, and a building costs four vertices per wall edge plus its roof (forty or more),
while a road ribbon costs eight vertices per segment of the way (hundreds for a long one). So a building
could start on a mesh with four vertices left, `addVertex` threw as it was designed to, and the throw
escaped `GameWorldRenderer.update()` and `QatarGame.render()`: the GL thread died and Android killed the
process with it. The previous two instalments never reached this code -- the country did not load at all
in round one, and in round two the bundle name stopped it before chunks were meshed -- so a bug that had
been sitting in the mesher for two rounds finally got a chance to run on a device.

Three rules now, and all three are needed:

  1. Every primitive is **sized before it is placed** (`MeshData.hasRoom(n)`, with the cost helpers on
     `BuildingMesher` and `RoadMesher` reported unclamped so "too big for any part" is a different answer
     from "fits the part I am about to use").
  2. A chunk's detail is a **list of parts** that roll over, up to three of them, instead of one mesh that
     fills up and stops -- so a dense chunk of Doha keeps its detail rather than losing its middle.
  3. Past that cap the mesher **stops cleanly and says so** (`ChunkMesh.truncated`) rather than throwing.
     A chunk missing a shed is a bug; a chunk that kills the app is worse.

**The one underneath it: the window was costing more memory than the phone would allow.** Every chunk
allocated three 4,096-vertex arrays -- 540 KB each, mostly empty -- and the resident window allowed 420
chunks: about 220 MB of geometry allocated before a single building was extruded, on top of 60 MB of
compiled country. A phone kills an app that does that without an exception, a stack trace or a chance to
say goodbye, which is one of the ways this round could have presented as an instant close. Meshes now
start small and grow, are trimmed to what they used, hand their CPU arrays over to the GPU and drop them,
and the window is bounded by a vertex budget (1,000,000) as well as by distance -- evicting the farthest
chunk rather than the one under the camera. Warm-up builds six chunks a frame instead of twenty-four.

**And the diagnostics, because a silent close must not be possible.** The crash journal is installed by
`AndroidLauncher` *before* libGDX is created -- the old recorder was created inside `create()`, one of
the things that can fail, and wrote through libGDX file handles, which need a context that a fatal
failure may never have produced. It writes breadcrumbs with the heap in use before every launch stage,
and on the next launch the app shows the previous run's record in a scrollable, selectable dialog and
starts the game only when a person has read it. Nothing deletes it: the old code printed the trace into
the panel and then deleted the file, so a repeating crash showed its reason for the fraction of a second
before it died again. `create()`, every frame, every launch stage and every chunk build are guarded now:
a failure is a sentence on the screen and a hole in the world, not a dead thread.

`ChunkMeshBudgetTest` meshes the densest chunk of the committed country at full detail -- found by
counting the footprints in every 500 m chunk of the real data, not guessed -- and holds every part inside
the index limit; `ChunkMeshCacheBudgetTest` holds the resident window inside its memory budget and checks
that the chunk taken back is the farthest one. Both would have failed on the code that shipped in round
two.

## The fourth run on a phone

The fourth install ran, which is progress -- the third closed instantly -- and it drew the fallback demo:
a sand-coloured plane and a cube, the panel over the top of it, and no controls. Three separate things
were wrong, and the third one had been wrong since the first line of the chunk mesher was written.

**The panel was hiding the game.** It took 78% of the screen height and it is drawn after the HUD, so with
anything to report it covered the world and the buttons. "No controls, nothing on the screen" is what a
screen looks like with the diagnostics on top of the controls. It is now a strip across the top of at most
42% of the screen, more transparent, at most ten lines, and when it has to drop a line it drops an
informational one rather than the failure.

**One broken subsystem took the whole screen with it.** `GameScreen.show()` was a single long sequence of
a dozen calls: whichever threw first abandoned the world, the controls and both maps, and left the demo
behind the panel. It is now built in independent parts -- raster, player and terrain, world renderer,
credits, tap-to-travel, map view, controls, camera -- each named, each reported, each survivable. The same
is true of a frame: the world and the controls draw under separate guards, the batch is closed in a
`finally`, and a frame that throws no longer disposes the game screen on the first exception (two seconds
of failing every frame is a broken subsystem; one bad frame is a hiccup). A failure is now raised on the
phone as a real dialog with a **Copy** button, while the game keeps running, so it can be sent on instead
of photographed.

**The chunks were drawn in the wrong place, always.** The meshers emit **bundle-space** coordinates --
metres from the compiled grid's origin -- because that is the frame the terrain, building and road data
are stored in. The renderer draws a chunk by translating it *from that chunk's own origin* into the
rebased frame next to the player, because it was written for **chunk-local** coordinates. Both are
reasonable conventions; nobody had ever compared them, and no test looked at a chunk's coordinates as
coordinates rather than as counts. So every chunk was drawn one origin-distance from its place: chunk
(221, 230) of Doha, whose origin is 110 km into the grid, appeared 110 km away from where it belonged,
and the country on the screen was mostly empty with a few squares of sand and half a building in it.

It now has one rule instead of four: whatever a mesher writes, a `ChunkMesh` is in chunk-local metres --
`MeshData.translate` moves the whole chunk into its own frame with its bounds, after the meshing and
before the trim. Geometry stays under 500 m, so a float still has centimetres of precision left at the far
end of a 259 km country. `ChunkMeshBudgetTest` now checks the numbers a driver actually receives -- every
float finite, every position inside its own chunk, every height inside a range Qatar has, every index
pointing at a vertex that exists, and one test that follows a real footprint from the compiled country to
the wall vertex standing on it.

## The fifth pass: an audit, after a report with no words in it

"Having some problems" is a complete bug report when the person holding the phone has already sent three
paragraphs and got a build that does not work. So this round was a read of every place where two systems
meet -- which is where the three bugs before this one all lived -- and it found three more.

**Every body was drawn at sea level.** `BodyMesher.transform` placed the player, the car, the aeroplane,
the helicopter and the boat at y = 0 and ignored the height entirely, while every body mesh is modelled
with y = 0 at what it stands on (feet, tyre contact patches, waterline, wheels) and the vehicle bodies keep
that contact height in `VehicleState.heightM`. A player walking across Doha at 10 m of elevation was under
the ground and invisible; a car on the sabkha was nowhere near its own shadow. The height is a required
argument of every transform now, so a caller cannot forget it, and a test places a body at 12 m and checks
its contact point, a shoulder and a wheel's axle.

**The depth buffer was 16 bits.** The world draws with a near plane of 0.25 m -- a first-person view has
to see its own feet -- and a far plane of 12 km, and it deliberately lifts roads, kerbs and markings by
centimetres so they do not fight with the terrain under them. At 16 bits the depth of a distant chunk
quantises to metres: terrain, roads and roofs interleave and shimmer as the camera moves. Every GLES 3
device this runs on has 24 bits available, and it asks for them now.

**The panel never went away.** As soon as anything had been logged as an error -- one failed chunk build,
one missing font -- it stayed on the screen for the rest of the session. Errors have an age: the panel
reports for thirty seconds after the last one and then gets out of the way. Nothing is lost, because the
failure is in the journal and the next launch's dialog shows it in full.

Reviewed and found correct, which is worth writing down because "I checked" is not evidence: the y-down
touch conversion against the y-up HUD layout, the map raster's south-first rows against SpriteBatch's
texture coordinates, the tap-to-travel chain from a screen pixel through the raster fraction to bundle
metres and back to the rebased frame the player moves in, the minimap marker against the drawn image, the
chunk-local convention against the renderer's per-chunk translation (the previous round's fix), the
locomotion switch re-uploading the body mesh, the streaming radius against the camera's far plane, and the
city spawn. A map that failed to build is now a sentence on the screen with a reason rather than a button
that quietly does nothing.

## Step three, finished: roofs and a person who walks

Two more pieces of detail, both visible from every camera and neither of them costing geometry:

  - **Roofs.** The mesher writes each roof vertex's offset from the roof centre in metres, where it used
    to write 0..1, and the fragment shader draws on it: gravel and bitumen, the seams between precast
    planks every 2.4 m, a plant housing and a galvanised water tank on a 6 m grid with the position
    hashed from the cell so the roofs of a city do not all look printed. It is the view from an aeroplane,
    on 190,000 buildings, and a test checks the offsets are metres on a footprint that is not the unit
    square a 0..1 range would fake.
  - **The person walks.** The legs and arms are moving parts now instead of one static box. Each limb is
    built around its own joint -- the same convention a wheel follows around its axle -- and swings
    through an amplitude driven by the body's spin phase: legs in opposite phase to each other, arms
    counter to the leg on the same side, the phase advancing at two steps per cycle, so a person at
    1.5 m/s takes a step every half second and stands still with still feet. `Part` gained a swing
    amplitude for this; a wheel, a propeller and a rotor still turn all the way round, so the machinery
    stayed one type rather than two.

## The workflow that started itself

`data.yml` publishes its provenance into `data/geodata/**` and had that same directory among its push
triggers, so every run published a commit that started the next run: five full country compiles were
queued that way, each of them re-downloading the OSM extract, recompiling 186,993 buildings and rewriting
the same evidence files. The trigger now fires for the compiler and for the workflow itself, and the job
refuses a head commit carrying the skip marker as well, because those commits arrive through the contents
API. Nothing about the compiled world changed -- same compiler, same sources, same bundles -- and the run
after the fix was the last one in the chain.

## The sixth report on a phone: "f != java.lang.Integer"

The panel said, in the app's own words, **`Failed the controls`** with an
`IllegalFormatConversionException: f != java.lang.Integer` under it -- six frames of
`java.util.Formatter`, a healthy heap, and a launch log that had reached "Starting the game screen"
without a complaint. Everything in that report was true and none of it named the cause.

The cause was one specifier. The HUD's status line was built inside the frame loop as

    String.format(Locale.ROOT, "%s | %.0f, %.0f | %s | %.0f fps", ..., Gdx.graphics.getFramesPerSecond())

and `Graphics.getFramesPerSecond()` returns an `int`. Java's formatter will not print an Integer
fractionally; it throws. The status line is drawn in the "controls" half of the frame, so the whole half
failed -- every frame, sixty times a second -- and after 120 of them the game gave up on drawing the
screen and left the panel up. That is also what the fourth report ("having some problems") most likely
was: the same exception, with the panel then staying on screen for the whole session instead of leaving
after thirty seconds, so the symptom read as "no controls, nothing".

**Four things were done about it, and the first is the one that lasts.**

1. The line is no longer inside the frame loop. `hud/StatusText.english(...)` is a pure function -- text
   in, text out, no GL, no `Gdx` -- so a test can reach it. It was unreachable before: the only way to
   try that line was to run the game on a phone. `StatusTextTest` asserts the whole line, the frame
   count as a count, metres rounded to the metre, negative coordinates, and -- deliberately -- that
   `%.0f` against an `int` throws `f != java.lang.Integer`, so the reason for the `%d` is a test and not
   a comment. The repaired call site is `%d`.
2. `boot/StackTraceText` chooses the alert's frames: the game's own first, the framework's if we are not
   in the throw, the JVM's only if there is nothing else, with a `(+N more, the JDK's own, in the
   record)` line for what was left out. The dialog that reported this failure spent all six of its lines
   on the printer. `ScreenLog` uses the same choice for the one frame the panel has room for.
3. `tools/check_format_specifiers.py` reads every `String.format` on a literal in the tree and reports a
   specifier that cannot print the value it is given -- and only when the value's kind is *provable*
   (a literal, a declared local, parameter or field, a cast, arithmetic, an unqualified call to a method
   declared here with one return type, or a short hand-checked list of library accessors that includes
   `getFramesPerSecond`). Unknown kinds are never reported; a format built by concatenation is skipped by
   design. Thirteen self-tests run before it looks at the tree, including the line that cost the sixth
   install and the four shapes that a text-matching draft got wrong. It found the real bug when it was
   put back, and the one true positive it reports in the committed tree is the test that asserts the
   refusal, which says so with a `format-gate: intentional` marker.
4. `VERSION` is `0.1.0-m0+diag6`, so the phone can tell this build from the one that failed.

The honest summary of the sixth install: nothing was wrong with the country, the chunks, the shaders or
the controls -- a text format string was wrong, in the one place in the project that no test could reach.

## The seventh report on a phone: the country, and nothing else

The report was "severe problems, also no controls" -- and, this time, a screenshot of the world drawing
perfectly behind it. No exception, no panel, no buttons, no minimap. Everything the previous six rounds
had fixed stayed fixed, and the controls were still missing, which meant the cause had never been the
format string or the font or the map.

It was GL state. The world renderer turns `GL_CULL_FACE` and `GL_DEPTH_TEST` on at the start of every
frame and never turns them off. libGDX's `SpriteBatch.begin()` -- from its own source, 1.12.1 -- sets
`glDepthMask(false)` and nothing else: it does not touch depth testing or culling. And SpriteBatch emits
a sprite as (x,y), (x,y+h), (x+w,y+h), (x+w,y), indexed 0-1-2, 2-3-0, which is **clockwise** in a y-up
coordinate system, while the world renderer leaves `glFrontFace(GL_CCW)` behind. Clockwise triangles
with a counter-clockwise front are back faces. With culling on, every quad the HUD, the minimap and the
panel drew was discarded by the GPU before it reached the screen.

That is why the reports kept coming back wordless: the app's own diagnostics were being culled too, and
the only channel that ever worked was the Android dialog, which is not GL. It is also why this survived
six rounds: it is correct code, it is invisible on a desktop where nothing leaves culling enabled, and
no test here owns a GPU.

**Four changes, and the first is the one that matters.**

1. `render/GlState2D.beginUi()` -- disable depth testing, disable back-face culling, depth writes off --
   called by `HudRenderer.begin()` and by the diagnostics panel's own draw. The 2D code sets the state
   it needs instead of inheriting whatever 3D left behind.
2. `WorldRenderer.endFrame()` hands the state back when a 3D pass is over, and `drawActor` sets its own
   3D state first (the body is drawn after the chunks) so handing it back cannot flatten the player.
3. `tools/check_ui_state.py`, the sixth source gate: every method that opens a `SpriteBatch` must call
   `GlState2D.beginUi()` in the same method first. Eight self-tests, including the exact shape of the
   bug; it reports the real omission when the call is taken out again.
4. `VERSION` is `0.1.0-m0+diag7`.

The mistake the gate encodes is worth naming: it is not a bug in a line of code, it is a *contract*
between two subsystems that neither of them owned. Nothing here can run GL, so the contract is now
written down in one place and enforced at the source.


## The eighth report: it crashed on the minimap, and the buildings had no roofs

Three things came back at once, and they were unrelated: opening the map closed the app, the buildings
looked like walls, and the physics needed checking. Each had a cause that the code could be asked about.

### The crash: teardown that touched GL

The map was opened, something in the frame threw, and the frame guard did what it was built to do after
two seconds of failures -- it dropped the game screen and fell back to the panel. Dropping it means
disposing it, and disposing touches GL: `worldRenderer.dispose()` frees meshes, `hud.dispose()` ends the
SpriteBatch. `SpriteBatch.end()` is where it dies: it calls `glDepthMask(...)`, `Gdx.gl` is null by then,
and the app is gone. It was not the map that crashed the game. It was the cleanup, called from the one
place in the program that cannot vouch for the GL context: a catch block, on the GL thread, after the
subsystem it is tearing down has already failed.

The fix is not a null check on the map. It is:

* `QatarGame.glAvailable()` -- is there a context, and are we on its thread -- and a teardown that takes
  that answer as an argument,
* the teardown moved **out of the catch block** and into `renderFrame`, where the frame guard already
  covers it and the context is current,
* `HudRenderer.dispose(boolean)` and `GameScreen.dispose(boolean)`, which leak the GL objects rather than
  call into a context that is not there. A leak at that point is invisible; a crash is not.

There is also a plain lesson about the order of operations: `begin()` was made safe for a null context in
round seven and `end()` was not, and `end()` runs in a `finally`.

### The buildings: a roof facing down is not a roof

  "make buildings correctly, I mean fill them, they look like walls"

They were walls. Extruded footprints, four sides, nothing on top -- and the cause was a handedness
mistake in the roof winding. The walls were right; every roof was wound so its front face pointed down
into the building and its back face at the sky. The world is drawn with `glCullFace(GL_BACK)`, so every
roof in the country was discarded: a building seen from above was a hole you could look down into, and
from the street it was four walls with nothing on top.

The rule, now written down in one place and tested against a real cross product: this is a right-handed
system with x east, y up and z north, so `x * z = -y`, the (east, north) plane is *left-handed*, and a
triangle wound counter-clockwise in it faces **down**. Roofs must be clockwise. Measured on the country:
185,551 of 186,993 rings arrive counter-clockwise, so almost every roof in Qatar was missing and the
1,442 that happened to come the other way were fine -- which is why it looked like a quirk of one
neighbourhood rather than a rule.

Two more things came out of the same pass, both found by writing the test first:

* The shaped roofs (gable, shed, dome, pyramid) were approximations that left **holes**: each ring edge
  contributed one triangle to the nearest ridge end, and those triangles do not tile a rectangle, let
  alone an L-shaped villa. Every building now gets a complete ear-clipped **deck** at the eave line, and
  the shaped cap is drawn above it -- so a roof that says "gabled" is a deck with a ridge on top, not a
  tent with gaps in it.
* The vertex budget was `8 * ring + 24`, sized for walls plus one roof. A deck plus a cap is more, so it
  is `10 * ring + 32`; an underestimate there is a primitive running off the end of an index buffer, which
  is how the third install died.

### The physics: two bugs the tests had never asked about

  "check all locomotion physics properly"

The bodies themselves were already covered by thirteen behaviour tests. What was not covered was the
seam, and two real bugs were sitting in it:

1. **Pulling the stick back walked the player forwards.** The direction of travel already carried the
   sign of the walk, and the speed was negative in that same case, so the movement code flipped the
   direction a second time and the two cancelled out. A one-line sign error that reads as broken physics.
2. **A walker could not jump at all, and the same flag bunny-hopped.** The jump was `climb > 0.5` -- the
   UP button's held value -- and UP is the flying machines' altitude control, disabled while walking. So
   the person could never jump, and the value was a *held* one, which would have repeated the jump on
   every landing. A jump is now a discrete request from its own **JUMP** button, buffered for 50 ms so a
   frame that runs no physics step cannot lose it, and consumed once.

And two things were verified rather than changed, because they are the conventions a "the physics is
wrong" report usually turns out to be about: every body's mesh points along +Z and the transform turns
that into the heading (0 north, 90 east) that the bodies move along, and every body's lowest point is its
own contact plane -- wheels, legs, skids, hull draft and all. `BodyFacingTest` measures both, by turning
the mesh's nose through the renderer's own matrix and comparing it with the distance the body actually
travelled.

### Also in this round

* **The minimap's tap target was its own 100-odd-pixel square** on a phone held at arm's length. The
  drawn square is unchanged; the target is now the whole top-right corner -- two fifths of the screen,
  with no button inside it, and never reaching the movement stick, which is checked at six resolutions.

### The ninth report: heat, immobility, the map, and a helicopter

Four complaints, and for once none of them were about the country: **"problem with human car physics,
cant move"**, **"heliopter flies but not perfectly"**, **"also minmap crashes now also"**, and the one
that reframed the whole round -- **"most importantly mobile becomes very hot after playing for a few
seconds"**.

**The heat first, because it was the one that made the game unplayable on a phone.** Four causes, all of
them in the frame loop:

* **No frame cap anywhere.** libGDX renders as fast as the device will let it, and every frame here
  meshes, culls, draws a country and updates a HUD. `QatarGame` now asks for `setForegroundFPS(60)`:
  16.6 ms a frame is the budget a screen can actually show.
* **Every resident chunk was drawn.** `ChunkCulling` now tests the chunk's bounding sphere against the
  camera's own `Frustum` (and against a top-of-box height), because a chunk behind the camera is still
  transformed and set up before the clipper throws it away, and with a 72-degree field of view on a phone
  most of a six-kilometre disc is behind the camera or off the side.
* **The streaming cache rebuilt the country for ever.** It meshed up to three chunks a frame, then evicted
  the farthest chunks to stay inside its memory budget -- and the chunks it had just evicted were, of
  course, still near the player, so they came straight back onto the build list. Build, evict, rebuild:
  three chunk meshes a frame, sixty times a second, six hundred country meshes a minute on the GL thread,
  for geometry the frame never got to keep. The cache now stops building while its window is full (the
  ground under the player's feet is the one exception), works on parallel arrays instead of allocating a
  few hundred candidate objects a frame, and evicts the chunk *farthest from the player* rather than the
  one inserted first -- which was wherever they came from.
* **The draw distance adapts.** `GameWorldRenderer` watches the measured frame time and shrinks the draw
  radius by 45 m a frame when frames run past 20.8 ms, recovering by 15 m when they run under 16.1 ms, and
  the diagnostics panel prints the millisecond figure the decision was made from. A phone that is
  struggling is now visible on the screen, in numbers, instead of only in the temperature of the handset.

**"The human and the car cannot move."** Two causes, and the first one is the one no test had asked about:
`WorldSurface.solidAt` decided *"am I inside a building?"* with the rectangle around each footprint, and a
rectangle is not a building. Measured against the compiled country: of 279 car spawn points, **52 (18.6%)
are inside some building's box and outside every wall**; of a sample of 43,287 road vertices, **1,029
(2.4%)** are the same. A walker or a car in one of those places is refused in every direction by its own
collision rule, with a panel that reports a speed of zero and no reason. `solidAt` now ray-casts the
building's own ring -- the polygon that is drawn is the polygon that is solid -- keeping the rectangle only
as a cheap reject. The second cause is that a spawn can still land inside a real wall (14 of the 279), so
`SpawnClearer` searches outward for clear ground before the player is placed, and a teleport goes through
the same search: a tap is a hope, not a coordinate.

**"The minimap crashes."** The big map's crop region had never been given a texture: `HudRenderer.drawBigMap`
called `setRegion(...)` on an empty `TextureRegion` and then drew it, which is a `Texture.getWidth()` on a
null. It is textured at construction now, guarded (a map that cannot be drawn draws nothing and says so
once, on the panel, in both languages), and `tools/check_ui_state.py` fails on that exact line shape, so
the bug cannot come back quietly.

**"The helicopter flies but not perfectly."** Also not one bug:

* the hover trim ignored the disc's tilt and the air's density, so a machine asked to hold 25 m coasted to
  43 m and stayed there -- the trim is now the machine's own arithmetic (`collectiveForAccel`), which
  answers "what collective holds *this* machine up at *this* height, speed and attitude?";
* the altitude hold *released itself* within a metre of the commanded height, with whatever climb rate it
  had built, which is what the coasting was -- it is a critically damped spring-damper now (a four-second
  period), and it stays engaged until the pilot takes the lever;
* the cyclic's sign was inverted, so pulling the stick forward flew the machine backwards at 29 km/h;
* and the flying stick was a *nudge*, not a stick: the deflection decayed back to trim at 1.6 a second, so
  a thumb held still for two seconds flew a machine that had already levelled itself out. It is a held
  stick now (`setStick`/`releaseStick`), drawn at rest on the right-hand side with its own word under it,
  for the same reason the movement stick is: a control that has to be discovered is not a control.

**Also in this round**, the three items carried from the last one:

* **Palms and vegetation.** `VegetationMesher` scatters palms (96 vertices) and scrub (24) in the
  landuse areas that say they are vegetation, with their own surface type (7) and their own branch in the
  terrain shader: the trunk is a tapered prism, the crown is a ring of fronds, and the whole thing fades
  out with the chunk's detail so that nothing is drawn at 500 m that reads as a smudge.
* **Vehicle-to-vehicle contact.** `VehicleContact` treats the machines the player is not in as solid: a
  walker bumps off a car's flank, a car is stopped by a parked aeroplane, and the panel says `BLOCKED`
  instead of "the speed is zero". The five machines are parked in a line behind the player at a spawn, a
  teleport or a vehicle change, each one nose to tail with the next: an arc cannot hold an 11-metre
  aeroplane and a 9-metre helicopter without overlapping them, and a line never crosses the direction the
  player is about to drive.
* **Streaming profiling.** The panel prints chunks resident, drawn, culled, draw calls, triangles,
  vertices against the budget, the frame time in milliseconds and the fog distance, all from counters the
  renderer keeps anyway.

### What this round cost, and what it taught

The compile errors the gate caught are the most useful thing the round produced:

1. **`Math.SQRT2` does not exist in Java.** `java.lang.Math` declares exactly two constants, `E` and
   `PI`; `SQRT2` is JavaScript's, and it was written into `ChunkCulling` from memory. The symbol gate now
   reads every `Math.` member in the tree against the members the JDK actually declares, names the six
   JavaScript roots people reach for (`SQRT2`, `SQRT1_2`, `LN2`, `LN10`, `LOG2E`, `LOG10E`) and says what
   to write instead: `Math.sqrt(2.0)`. The failure mode is worth remembering -- *it does not type-check to
   remember a library, and the local gates cannot tell a wrong member from a right one*.
2. **An `@Override` drifted off its method.** The new `insideFootprint` was inserted between the annotation
   and `materialId`, so the annotation landed on a private method and javac refused it. The gate now fails
   on three shapes of that mistake: an annotation whose next line is a comment (a Javadoc between an
   annotation and its method documents nothing and moves the annotation), an annotation followed by
   anything with no parameter list, and an annotation on a `private` method, which can never override
   anything.

Both rules were run against the broken revision before they were trusted (`git show <commit>:<path>` into
a temporary file, then through the gate), and both reproduce the CI errors exactly. The round's other
lesson is smaller and older: *a test that keeps what it builds is measuring the heap*. The streaming test
walked fifteen hundred frames and pinned every chunk mesh it built in a map nothing read; the meshes are
released now, as the renderer releases them.

### What this round cost, and what it taught

Three pushes, two of them red, and both failures were the project's own rather than the phone's:

1. `Gdx.graphics.isGLThread()` does not exist. It was written into `glAvailable()` from memory, and javac
   refused it -- run `38788120436` -- because there is no such method in libGDX 1.12.1 (checked against
   libGDX's own source afterwards: not in 1.13.0 either). The context question libGDX can answer is
   `Gdx.gl != null`, which is what the method asks now, and the thread question is answered by *where* the
   teardown runs: inside `renderFrame()`, which is the GL thread by definition. Ask the library; do not
   remember it.
2. `ChunkMeshBudgetTest` asserted the old roof arithmetic -- `vertexCost == 64` -- instead of the bound the
   number exists to enforce, so round eight's roof change failed a test that was describing the mesher
   wrongly. It now meshes a five-sided ring once per roof shape and asserts the reported cost covers every
   vertex the mesher then writes, which is the property that keeps a building from running off the end of
   an index buffer. It was corrected, not relaxed: an assertion that pins arithmetic instead of a promise
   breaks every time the thing it describes legitimately changes.

Both were caught by CI before any phone saw them, and the local-name gate caught two shadowed locals in
the same new code -- the gates are the compiler here, and they have to be re-run after the *last* edit, not
after the first one.

### The tenth report: a teleport that arrived somewhere else, and two bodies that would not move

**The report:** "minimap teleport may be broken, teelport happens abnormmally or crashes , fix that and

**The diagnosis, and it is the one thing about a floating origin that is easy to get half right.** The

report is about the map; but the same runaway explains "cant walk, use car", because a walker's and a car's

symptoms, one cause, and it was invisible from inside the game because everything still looked like the

* A tap that is not on the map is refused (`MapView.containsPoint`) with a message instead of resolving to

### The eleventh report: keep building — a horizon where the fog was, and three holes

**Three faults of the same shape: geometry, or a code path, that nothing owned.**

1. **The coastline of the streamed world was a hole.** The land pass drew a cell only when all four of its
   corners were land and the water pass only when all four were water, so every cell straddling the shore
   was drawn by neither -- a band up to two cells wide, two hundred metres at 100 m cells, of nothing at
   all along every coast, with the sky behind it. A cell now belongs to the layer its own flag names, and
   both layers take a lattice point's height from one rule, so the beach and the sea share an edge.
   `MeshersTest.coastlineHasNoGap` counts the cells across both layers and checks the shared column.
2. **The world ended in fog at five kilometres.** `HorizonMesh` builds the national terrain at 300 m a cell

   haze. `HorizonMeshTest` asserts the ring covers every cell, faces up, carries the country's heights and

3. **A tap could close the app and the crash reporter never heard about it.** In libGDX 1.12.1,

   the handler, and the game carries on.

**And a new kind of answer to "cant walk":** after two seconds of asking and going nowhere, a body that is

seconds.

The user asked, again, for the work to continue -- "keep building" -- and the loop this round had nothing
from the phone to go on, so the round was spent reading what the last installs had actually left behind.

**The city stopped at the detail radius.** The streamed chunks carry real buildings to 2.5 km and the
eleventh install's horizon carries the country from 6 km to 11.4 km; between the two there was nothing, so
